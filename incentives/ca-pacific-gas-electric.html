What this utility pays today

All four measures can be proposed under PG&E Calculated Incentives (Custom Retrofit): the compressor-cycling controller, the refrigerant-side treatment of existing cooling equipment, rooftop unit replacement and the solar thermal system with storage1. The rate card prints $75.00 per kW, $0.06 per kWh and $0.50 per therm for savings to code or standard practice, and $150.00 per kW, $0.12 per kWh and $1.25 per therm for savings above code1. A written yes from the utility comes first: the site prequalifies before anything is bought or installed, and no incentive agreement is signed until the CPUC's custom project review releases it1.

For a rooftop unit that failed or was due for replacement anyway, only savings above standard practice count, so the above-code rate is the one in play2. For the solar thermal system, PG&E's rulebook treats solar-powered technology that generates no electricity as eligible, subject to CPUC acceptance3.

The demand charge

On PG&E's B-10 rate, the medium general demand-metered service for sites expected at 75 to 499 kW with no minimum demand requirement, the demand charge totals $20.50 for each kW of the highest demand in the billing month, in summer and winter alike4. That total covers distribution, transmission and reliability services, with generation printed as a dash4. A demand charge bills the highest kW the site pulls in a billing period, and a rooftop unit's compressor starts add to that peak. The sheet does not carry a ratchet, so on a 10-ton rooftop unit each month's peak stands on its own4.

Demand response

The Base Interruptible Program (E-BIP) from PG&E pays a monthly credit on the load a site commits to drop, at $10.60/kW from November to April and $13.60/kW from May to October for the 15 minute option at 1 kW to 500 kW, if the site can commit at least 100 kW of reduction5. A customer may enroll directly with PG&E or through a demand response aggregator5. It pays for load reduction, and it is not an equipment rebate.

Financing

California's C-PACE law is in force, and it finances renewable generation and improvements that cut energy or water use, as long as they are permanently fixed to the property6. Each city or county opts in on its own, for example by joining CSCDA Open PACE and adopting a resolution6. Because it is decided county by county and city by city, we don't print our current count here, and we confirm it for the address6.
